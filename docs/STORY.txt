  PART 1   THE PROBLEM, AS A STORY
================================================================================

  THE CHARACTERS

    PRIYA     an on-call engineer.  Competent.  Tired.  It is 3am.
    THE BOSS  wants to know why it took 40 minutes to find the cause.
    THE AGENT the AI assistant we are building.


  SCENE:  3:04 AM.  A PAGE FIRES.

  Something called MS_5261 is slow.  Priya has never heard of MS_5261.
  There are thousands of services.

  Here is what happens TODAY, without any of this:


    3:04   Priya opens the dashboard.  MS_5261 is red.  So are
           forty other things.  Which one is the cause and which
           ones are just downstream?  The dashboard cannot say.
           It shows numbers, not connections.

    3:11   She opens the tracing tool.  She can see one slow
           request.  She cannot see the shape of the whole system.

    3:19   She asks in chat: "does anyone know what MS_5261 talks
           to?"  Nobody answers.  It is 3am.

    3:26   She finds a wiki page.  It was last edited 14 months
           ago.  Two of the services on it no longer exist.

    3:33   She guesses that the problem is the node underneath,
           and she is right.  But she cannot prove it, and she
           does not know who owns the service, so she pages the
           wrong team.

    3:44   Forty minutes gone.  The actual fix takes four.


  WHY IT TOOK 40 MINUTES

  Not because Priya is slow.  Because the information she needed was
  split across four places that do not know about each other:

        the dashboard knows NUMBERS but not CONNECTIONS
        the tracing tool knows ONE REQUEST but not the SYSTEM
        the wiki knows WORDS but they are OUT OF DATE
        nothing at all knows WHO OWNS WHAT

  And crucially:  nothing could tell her what the system looked like
  AT 3:04.  Everything shows her what it looks like NOW, at 3:33,
  after things have already moved.

  That last one is the real killer.  By the time you look, the evidence
  has changed.


  THAT IS THE PROBLEM.  All four layers exist to fix one scene.

================================================================================
  PART 4   THE THREE MOMENTS THAT LAND
================================================================================

  A demo is remembered for about three things.  These are ours.


  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  BEAT 1    MOVE THE SLIDER.  THE ANSWER CHANGES.
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Show the graph picture.  Ask for the blast radius of NODE_12426.

      At 00:01:00  ->  2,591 services affected
      At 00:02:30  ->  2,055 services affected

  Drag the time slider between the two.  The picture visibly redraws.

  Then say:

      "Both of those are correct.  The system changed in between.
       Your current dashboard can only ever show you NOW.
       At 3am, NOW is already the wrong answer."

  WHY THIS LANDS:  everyone in the room has lost time to this exact
  problem.  Nobody has ever seen a tool fix it.


  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  BEAT 2    BREAK A RULE ON PURPOSE.  WATCH THE BUILD REFUSE.
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Open the ontology file in front of them.  It is readable YAML,
  not code.  Point at one line:

      a "calls" link must go from a Microservice to a Microservice

  Now deliberately make the builder produce one bad link — a service
  "calling" a database.  Run the build.

  It stops.  It prints the rule that failed, the count, and five
  example rows.

  Then say:

      "That is one line of a config file refusing to let bad data
       through.  Without it, that link would still be in the graph.
       Nothing would crash.  Every query would just quietly return
       slightly the wrong answer, forever."

  Then land the real number:

      "This is not hypothetical.  When we turned these rules on, they
       found 34,594 links pointing at the wrong kind of thing, and
       10,969 pointing at IDs that never existed.  In OUR OWN CODE.
       No query would have shown us that."

  WHY THIS LANDS:  it turns the most abstract layer into the most
  concrete one.  People stop asking what an ontology is for.


  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  BEAT 3    THE SAME SCREEN.  THE BILL IS ZERO.
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Last.  Put the two side by side — the paid platform and ours,
  answering the same question with the same data.

  Then show the bill.

      Paid platform    $<X> per month, running this workload
      Our free stack   $0

      No account.  No credit card.  No data leaving the building.

  And then, immediately, the honest part:

      "Here is where paying is still the right answer: <list it>."

  WHY THAT LAST LINE MATTERS:  a comparison where free wins everything
  reads like a sales deck and gets discounted.  One that names its own
  limits gets believed.  We give up the perfect score on purpose,
  because it buys us the credibility of everything else.



================================================================================
  PART 5   THE STORY WE TELL THE MANAGER
================================================================================

  Three acts.  About four minutes if spoken.


  ACT 1 — SOMEONE ALREADY SOLVED THIS, FOR MONEY
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

      "There is a version of this built inside Databricks.  All four
       layers, plus the charts.  It works.  We are not claiming it
       does not.

       It also bills by the hour, and the definitions live inside
       that platform.  Move off it and you rewrite them."


  ACT 2 — SO WE BUILT IT FOUR TIMES
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

      "We did not build one alternative and declare victory.
       We built the same four layers four times, on the same 13
       million rows, answering the same five questions:

            once in Databricks        (the paid reference)
            once in Snowflake         (the other paid option)
            once with free tools      (Neo4j, Qdrant, Grafana)
            once with nothing at all  (just SQL and Python)

       That last one is the control group.  It is how we can say
       what each platform ACTUALLY gave us, instead of what its
       marketing page says it gives us."


  ACT 3 — HERE IS WHAT WE LEARNED
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

      "Three things.

       One.  Neither Databricks nor Snowflake is a graph database.
       Both of them do the graph layer by putting rows in a table
       and walking them.  For that one layer, the free tool is not
       the cheap option — it is the better option.

       Two.  Free often has an asterisk.  dbt's metric layer is free
       to write and paid to serve.  Most free catalogues need Docker.
       One embedded graph database we looked at was bought by Apple
       and archived last year.  We can tell a customer exactly where
       those traps are, because we walked into them.

       Three.  The definitions are portable and the platforms are not.
       The same ontology file and the same metric file drove all four
       builds, unchanged.  That is the actual product."


  THE ONE LINE, IF THERE IS ONLY TIME FOR ONE

      "We can give a customer all four layers, on their own machine,
       for nothing — and we have the measurements to prove it is not
       a toy."

         Say the "NOW is already the wrong answer" line.

   3:00  PEEL IT BACK
         Show the PART 3 diagram.  Walk each arrow in one sentence.
         End on layer 1 and the "contributed nothing / reason it is
         not wrong" line.

   4:30  BEAT 2 — BREAK A RULE
         Open the YAML.  Break the link.  Run the build.
         Let it fail on screen.  Then give the 34,594 number.

   6:30  THE FOUR ARMS
         One slide.  The diagram from ARCHITECTURE section 6.
         "Same data.  Same five questions.  Four stacks."

   7:30  THE SCORECARD
         The filled-in table.  Let them read it.  Stay quiet.

   8:30  BEAT 3 — THE BILL
         The cost comparison.  Then immediately the "here is where
         paying still wins" list.

   9:30  WHAT WE WANT
         Say plainly what you are asking for — more time, a customer
         to pilot with, a decision.  Do not end on a summary.
         End on a request.


  RULES FOR THE DEMO

    - Never say "as you can see".  If they can see it, say nothing.
    - Never apologise for what is missing.  State it flatly or
      leave it out.
    - If something breaks, say "that is the live system, not a
      recording" and move on.  Do not debug in front of them.
    - Every number you say out loud must be in the scorecard.
      If it is not measured, do not say it.

  PART 7   WHAT IS REAL TODAY, AND WHAT WE STILL HAVE TO FIND
================================================================================

  Be strict about this.  The story above is written as if it all works.
  Some of it does not yet.  Marking the gap is how we avoid demoing a
  number we invented.


  ALREADY MEASURED AND TRUE
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    blast radius NODE_12426 at 00:01:00   =  2,591 services
    blast radius NODE_12426 at 00:02:30   =  2,055 services
    query time                            =  about 0.1 seconds
    rt_p99 for MS_5261                    =  18,361 ms
    the same p99 two independent ways     =  identical
    rows in the graph                     =  1.27M things, 1.52M links
    build time                            =  76 seconds
    bad links the rules caught            =  34,594 + 10,969
    hash collisions found and fixed       =  5,878 -> 0
    serious rule breaks now               =  zero


  STILL TO FIND — THESE ARE REAL TASKS, NOT WRITING
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

    [ ] A node that genuinely saturates, with services on it that
        genuinely slow down at the same minute.
        The whole story needs ONE real example.  If NODE_12426 does
        not have a real CPU spike, we find the node that does and we
        rewrite the story around it.  WE DO NOT FAKE IT.

    [ ] The "before" latency number for MS_5261.
        Marked <X> in PART 2.  Must be measured.

    [ ] The 14 co-located services that slowed together.
        Must be counted, not estimated.

    [ ] The matching post-mortem, and its real similarity score.
        Needs layer 3b built first.

    [ ] The team that owns MS_5261.
        Needs the derived teams checked against the actual graph.

    [ ] The monthly cost of the paid platform at this workload.
        Needs the Databricks arm actually run.  List price alone
        is not good enough to put on a slide.

    [ ] The list of cases where paying IS the right answer.
        We cannot write this honestly until all four arms are done.


  THE RULE:  every <X> and every empty bracket above must be filled
  with a measured number before this story is told to anyone outside
  the team.

  genuine saturation spike with genuine victims, we need a different
  slice — and that is a much cheaper thing to discover now than
  three weeks from now with the app already built around it.

  That is what a story plan is for.
